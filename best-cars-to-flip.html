Best cars to flip: how to pick one that sells

No single car is the best one to flip. The best car is one you can price, fix cheaply and sell to lots of buyers near you. This guide shows how to pick that car, what to skip, and how to check the rust and the title before you hand over cash.

What makes a car easy to flip

Look for cars that many people in your area want and can afford.

  • Common models. The more of a model there is on the road, the more buyers look for it. Parts and repairs are also easier to find.
  • Everyday body styles. Commuter sedans, small SUVs and work trucks suit most buyers.
  • A price most buyers can pay in cash. A cheaper car has more cash buyers.
  • Simple fixes only. A car that needs tires, a battery, a detail or small cosmetic work is easier to flip than one with engine or transmission trouble.
  • A clean title in the seller's name. See the title checks below.

Check local demand before you buy

A car that is popular across the country may still sell slowly in your town. Before you make an offer, search local listings for the same model, a close year, close miles and the same trim.

  • Count the similar cars for sale. A handful is a good sign. If there are none, you may not be able to price it. If there are dozens, you will be competing on price.
  • Watch how long they stay up. Listings that sit for weeks are usually priced too high, or the car is a hard sell.
  • Note the asking prices that keep coming up. That gives you a rough idea of what the car can sell for. It is an estimate, and it changes with your area and the time of year.

FlipMax works the same way. It aims for 5 similar cars. With 3 or 4 it shows a low confidence estimate, and with fewer than 3 it gives no estimate. If you cannot find 3 similar cars near you, you cannot price that car well. That is a good reason to pass.

Cars to skip when you start

  • Rare, exotic, classic and heavily modified cars. They have few buyers and few similar cars to compare against, so they are hard to price and can sit for a long time.
  • Salvage, rebuilt, flood or junk titles. Fewer buyers want them, and they are harder to price.
  • Warning lights, smoke, knocking or slipping gears. Any of these can turn a small flip into a big repair bill.
  • A seller who cannot show the title, or whose name does not match the one on it.
  • A car whose asking price is already above the most you should pay.

How to check for rust

Rust is easy to spot and costly to fix. Check in daylight and bring a flashlight.

  • Look under the car at the frame rails, floor pans and subframe. Light surface rust on bolts is common. Flaking metal, holes or soft spots mean walk away.
  • Check the rocker panels under the doors, the wheel arches, and the bottom edges of the doors and tailgate.
  • Lift the trunk floor mat and look for rust or standing water.
  • Bubbles under the paint usually mean rust underneath.
  • Check for flood signs. The FTC says to look for mud or sand under the seats or dash, rust around the doors, and loose, stained or mismatched carpet. A musty smell is another warning sign.

How to check the title

  • Match the VIN. The 17 character VIN on the title should match the one on the dashboard, at the lower left of the windshield, and the one on the driver's door jamb label.
  • Match the name. The person named on the title should be the person selling the car. Ask to see their ID.
  • Read the brands. Look for words like salvage, rebuilt, flood or junk printed on the title.
  • Ask about liens. If a lender is listed, the loan has to be paid off and released before the title can be signed over. Rules differ by state, so check with your state DMV.
  • Run a title history check. The federal NMVTIS site, vehiclehistory.gov, lists approved sellers of title history reports. NMVTIS records state brands like salvage and flood. That helps catch a car that was retitled in another state to hide its past. It does not show repair history, and it does not replace an inspection.
  • Check for open recalls. It is free with the VIN at nhtsa.gov/recalls.

Run the numbers last

Once a car passes these checks, work out the most you should pay. Count every cost: tax, title, repairs, detailing and selling. Use the free max bid calculator and the profit calculator. Then stick to your number. Walking away is part of the job.

Questions

What is the best car to flip for a beginner?

A common car that many local buyers want. It should have a clean title, need only simple fixes, and have plenty of similar cars listed near you. No one model is right for every area.

How do I know if a car will sell fast?

Look at similar listings near you. If there are several and they do not sit long, the demand is there. If similar cars stay listed for weeks, expect a slower sale or a lower price. Any guess at days to sell is an estimate.

Should I flip a car with a salvage or rebuilt title?

Not when you are starting out. Fewer buyers want branded titles, they are harder to price, and the damage history matters. Rules for these titles differ by state.

Is a clean title history report enough?

No. Not all damage gets reported, coverage can have gaps, and NMVTIS does not show repair history. Do your own rust and flood checks too, and get a mechanic to inspect the car if you can.
