How to find cars to flip

Most of a flip is decided before you hand over cash: the car you pick and what you pay for it. Here is a step by step way to find cars worth flipping, and to know when to walk away.

1. Pick a lane

Start with cars you know and lots of people want, like common commuter sedans, small SUVs and work trucks. They are easier to price and easier to sell. Set a price range you can pay in cash without stress. Skip rare, exotic, classic and heavily modified cars at first. They are hard to price and can sit for weeks.

2. Learn what your cars sell for

Know the finish line before you hunt. Look up similar cars for sale near you: same model, close year, close miles, same trim. Note the asking prices that keep showing up. Cars that sit a long time are usually priced too high. Write down a realistic "what it can sell for" number for the cars in your lane. It is an estimate, and it changes with your area and the time of year.

3. Search where private sellers list

Most flip cars come from private sellers, not lots.

  • Online marketplaces and local classifieds sites. Filter by your price range and sort by newest. Check in the early morning and the evening, when many people post.
  • Public auctions. Read the rules and fees first. You often cannot test drive.
  • Word of mouth. Tell mechanics, body shops, coworkers and family that you buy cars. People who are moving, downsizing or handling an estate often want a simple sale.
  • Driving around. "For sale" signs in car windows still turn up cars that are not listed online.

4. Read the listing like a flipper

Good signs: a seller who needs it gone ("moving", "bought a new car"), bad photos, a short description, or a fixable problem like a dead battery, worn tires or cosmetic damage. These things can keep other buyers away.

Warning signs: no title, a title not in the seller's name, a salvage or rebuilt title you do not understand, frame rust, a seller who will not let you see the car in person, or a price far below the rest with pressure to send money first. Skip those.

5. Work out the most you should pay before you message

Start with what it can sell for. Take away repairs, title and registration fees, cleaning, and the profit you want for your time. What is left is your max bid. Write it down so you do not talk yourself past it at the car. Some flips lose money, so plan for that. Our free max bid calculator does the math for you.

6. Keep the first message short

Your first message only asks to see the car. For example: "Hi, is it still available? Could I come see it today or tomorrow?" Do not offer a price yet. You have not seen what the listing left out.

7. Find what the listing missed

At the car, slow down. Check the oil and the coolant. Look for head gasket signs like milky oil or white smoke. Check tire wear, dents, rips, rust and paint that does not match. Make sure the VIN on the car matches the title. Our free Before You Buy checklist walks you through it. Then update your repair cost and your max bid.

8. Make your offer, and explain it

Open below your max bid and walk the seller through the repairs you found. Our rule of thumb, which is an estimate you adjust to the car: open about $150 to $250 under your max bid on cheaper cars, and about $400 to $500 under on pricier ones. Never go past the max bid. If the seller will not meet you, thank them and move on.

9. Check your state's rules

Many states limit how many cars you can sell in a year before you need a dealer license. The limit and the paperwork vary by state, so check with your state's motor vehicle office before you scale up. Sign titles over properly and keep a bill of sale for each car.

Questions

Where do people find cars to flip?

From private sellers on online marketplaces and local classifieds sites, at public auctions, and by word of mouth. New listings from private sellers are often where the better prices are, so check often.

How much should I pay for a car I plan to flip?

No more than your max bid. That is what it can sell for, minus repairs, fees and the profit you want. The free max bid calculator works it out. Its numbers are estimates.

Do I need a dealer license to flip cars?

It depends on your state. Many states cap how many cars one person can sell in a year without a license. Check with your state's motor vehicle office.

How does FlipMax estimate the most I should pay?

It compares the car with similar cars listed for sale near you. It aims for 5 similar cars. With 3 or 4 it shows a low confidence estimate. With fewer than 3 it gives no estimate. The result is always an estimate, not a promise of what the car will sell for.
